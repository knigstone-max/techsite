Some games take a paragraph to explain. Nocturnal 2 takes a sentence: you have a sword, the sword is on fire, and you can't let it go out. Everything else, the exploring, the fighting, the puzzles and even the story, grows out of that one small idea. It works far better than you'd expect.

It came out on Steam on November 10 with little fanfare, on the same day as several much bigger releases. That's a shame, because small Swiss studio Sunnyside Games has made exactly the kind of game that's easy to miss and worth finding.

## Where it comes from

The first Nocturnal, from 2023, was a short, linear platformer. It was polished, with good movement and combat, and mostly it left you feeling those mechanics deserved a bigger map. The sequel gives them one. Nocturnal 2 is a proper Metroidvania: one city, districts that link together, locked doors you come back to once you've earned a new ability, and secret passages everywhere.

## The story: a city gone cold

You play Ardeshir, a warrior crossing a world swallowed by a creeping mist that steals memories and homes. After a trek through a silent wasteland he reaches Ytash, a lost city on a forgotten island that once worshipped a never-ending flame. The Order of the Enduring Flame has broken apart, but the coals are still warm. Someone is supposed to come and light them again, and that's you.

The game doesn't tell its story in long cutscenes. It scatters it through notes, poems, statue inscriptions and short lines of text that pop up when you find something. Bit by bit a whole city comes together: alchemists who dedicated Ytash to the Sunbird, workers who fed the furnaces and sometimes gave their lives to them, and the fishermen and oil makers everyone looked down on even though the city ran on their fuel. There are flame dancers, artists who shaped fire and branded their own skin, and a group of moonstruck outcasts who drifted away into the depths.

Under all of that runs a quiet personal thread. Ardeshir carries the ashes of his sister, Arsia, along with the question of how far you'd go to bring someone back. Along the way you meet Yalda the Elder, another seeker of the flame, who offers a few careful words now and then and hints at what it can really do. When the story reaches the heart of the city it turns from a conquest into a story about grief and balance. It's a strong moment, though the final stretch gets a little vague about what you're meant to be doing.

## The flame is the game

The loop is simple: walk into an area, light every torch you can find, and watch the map fill in. Each torch raises the city's flame presence percentage, and that number is what drives progress. Some doors only open at certain thresholds: around 30% early on, then 70% and even 100% later. Finishing a district lights up that part of the city, and it feels good every time.

Torches aren't always within reach. Some you light with thrown knives, some through switches on tracks, and some hide behind tapestries or in small holes in the wall. Paying attention pays off.

The flame is a resource as much as a tool, though. Water puts out your sword, some hazards snuff it, and without fire your blade hits softer and you can't teleport. So you're always planning your route from torch to torch. That's what gives the game its rhythm: you're never just fighting or just jumping, you're always minding the fire. No extra meter, no cluttered HUD, just a sword that's burning or isn't.

![A Sun Child statue glowing warmly in a dark hall, with a tip explaining you can fast travel through the map to the Sun Child or any activated Phoenix](img/posts/nocturnal-2-sun-child.webp "The Sun Child: fast travel, upgrades and a place to rest. Screenshot: Sunnyside Games")

## Movement: fast from the start

One thing I really liked: Nocturnal 2 doesn't make you wait to move well. You start with a double jump, a dash and throwing knives. The toolkit grows from there: the Flame Lift, an upward burst that also launches enemies; a wall vault that keeps you airborne as long as you hit something; the Wing Strike, a downward kick from the air; a charged Sun Slash that breaks armour; Spinning Claws that power switches; and the Blazing Dive, a ground pound that opens cracked floors.

Each new ability arrives next to a spot where you can try it, which explains what it's for and sends you back to old areas with fresh ideas. Because the basic movement is strong from the outset, early exploring is a real pleasure. The flip side is that backtracking is shorter than the genre usually offers. If you love returning to an area hours later to find it's opened up completely, you'll get less of that here.

The controls are tight and fully remappable. Chaining jumps, wall vaults and air dashes feels fluid and precise, and controller support is complete. This is Nocturnal 2 at its best.

## Combat: fun, but static

Combat is all about speed and air play. You hit short combos, throw knives from range, pop an enemy up with the Flame Lift, keep hitting it in the air and slam it back down with the Wing Strike. Bigger enemies wear armour you have to break first. Later you get a parry, which gives fights a nice rhythm once you learn the timing, and a Furnace's Favor stance with a longer combo and heavier hits.

Healing comes from the Pyre Potion, which refills as you land hits, so the game rewards aggression. Your health bar is small and mistakes get punished fast, but checkpoints come often enough that it rarely stings.

![The hero mid-combo against an enemy, surrounded by orange sparks and fire in a dark hall](img/posts/nocturnal-2-fire-combat.webp "With the sword lit, everything moves fast. Screenshot: Sunnyside Games")

The problem is that combat at the end is almost the same as combat at the start. There's one weapon, the flaming sword, and upgrades add the odd extra hit to a combo. No new weapons, no different fighting styles. Enemy variety is thin, and some of them, especially the flying eyes and the homing-projectile throwers, soak up too many hits. Since your damage stays modest, those fights drag. One more small annoyance: the launch is tied to the jump button, so sometimes you send an enemy flying when you only wanted to hop.

## Boss fights: the best part

There are four bosses, and each one is excellent. Every boss has its own gimmick and phases where you break armour, survive a few attack patterns and go again. It's exactly where all the systems, movement, parry and flame, come together. Which is why it's frustrating there are only four. The map is big, and a few mini-bosses between districts would have broken up the routine.

![A boss fight against a dark red background: a huge burning figure charges, with a glowing red diamond symbol in the corner](img/posts/nocturnal-2-boss-fight.webp "One of the four bosses. Screenshot: Sunnyside Games")

## Puzzles, map and upgrades

Puzzles are on the light side and lean on platforming and observation rather than head-scratching. Timed braziers switch waterfalls on and off, moving hazards need sharp timing, oil-soaked rooms test your jumps, and diamond-shaped torches only stay lit for a moment. Water and darkness are part of the puzzle too. Near the end there's a set of doors that only open if you put out your own flame on purpose. It's a clever idea that ties into the theme, but it shows up with almost no introduction after hours of being told to protect your fire at all costs.

The map deserves praise. It marks torches you've passed but haven't lit, notes spikes on its own and puts icons on sealed doors that remind you what each one needs. Every area has a counter for torches and treasures, and you can add as many markers as you like. Phoenix statues are checkpoints that also heal, and you can fast travel from the map to any activated statue or to a Sun Child. Backtracking never becomes a slog.

There's no experience grind. Upgrades come from Cinderhearts, a currency found at set spots in the world rather than dropped by enemies, which you spend at a Sun Child to rekindle brands: a sprint, more health, a third hit in the air combo, knives that make torches explode, stronger melee at low health and more. Cinderhearts are limited, so early choices matter. It's a pity you can't see exactly what a brand does before paying, or swap brands freely.

## How it looks and sounds

This is where Nocturnal 2 is simply beautiful. The hand-drawn art draws on Persian architecture: domes, arches, tiled halls and layered rooftops set against deep darkness. It gives Ytash a clear identity, far from generic fantasy. Each district has its own palette, and fire is the star. It lights most of the world, reflects off surfaces, and even the darkest rooms stay readable.

![A golden palace dome on a cliff under a purple, misty sky, with the hero walking along a stone bridge below](img/posts/nocturnal-2-dome-cliff.webp "Ytash from outside. Screenshot: Sunnyside Games")

The sound fits: sombre, slightly haunted music that changes between areas without fighting the action, and the crackle of fire underneath. There's full English voice acting, and the lore text that appears beside each find adds to the mood. If you're sensitive to it, note that even with camera shake turned off, a slight shake on jumps and a mild background blur remain.

The requirements are tiny: a Core i5-6600, a GTX 750 Ti, 8GB of RAM and 3GB of storage. Nearly any home PC will run it. There's no Hebrew, and players with basic English may struggle with some of the poems and notes, but you can play without catching every word.

## Length and three endings

The story takes around four to six hours. Chasing every secret and all three endings easily pushes that past ten. You can't see all three endings in one run, so you'll need several playthroughs. The true ending means following the small blue eye symbols that hint at deeper secrets.

Two quick warnings. First, once you reach the heart of the city the map disappears, checkpoints stop working, and you head back through familiar halls putting out flames. It closes the story nicely, but with no map and waves of tough enemies it's disorienting the first time. Second, and more important: one ending wipes your save file when it's done. It suits a game about fire, but back up your save first.

## The verdict

Nocturnal 2 knows exactly what it is: a small, fast, beautiful Metroidvania with one great idea holding the whole thing up. It doesn't compete with the genre's giants and doesn't try to. Combat needed more depth and a few more bosses would have helped. But at ₪32.99, and ₪29.69 until November 19, it's hard to find a reason not to try it. That's less than lunch at the mall.

A Switch version was announced back in 2024, but for now it has no date. If you want to play on the go, you'll have to keep waiting, or use a laptop.

:::score
## The bottom line

8/10

A hand-drawn Persian world you can't stop looking at, fluid movement from minute one, a flame mechanic that gives everything a rhythm and four excellent boss fights. On the other hand, combat barely changes, there aren't enough enemies or bosses to fill the map, and the final stretch is confusing. A short, polished game at a price that makes it an easy recommendation for anyone who likes the genre.
:::

**What I liked**

- Gorgeous hand-drawn, Persian-inspired art
- A flame mechanic that ties exploring and fighting together
- Fast, fluid movement from the start
- Four excellent boss fights
- A smart map and fast travel that cut the walking
- Very low price and modest requirements

**What I didn't**

- One weapon and combat that hardly evolves
- Few bosses and limited enemy variety
- The map-less final stretch is confusing
- One ending deletes your save
- Windows only for now, no Hebrew
