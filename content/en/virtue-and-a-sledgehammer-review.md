Some games open with a tutorial. This one opens with a hammer. Virtue and a Sledgehammer puts a heavy sledge in your hands and sends you to the town you grew up in so you can wreck it. Not because anyone invaded, not because of zombies. Because it isn't yours anymore, and maybe it never was.

It comes from Deconstructeam, the small Spanish studio behind The Red Strings Club and The Cosmic Wheel Sisterhood, working with Selkie Harbour, and Devolver Digital is publishing. It came out on October 27 for Windows only, and since then it's been one of the most talked-about indie games around. There's a good reason. It's an angry, blunt and at times exhausting game about grief, about family, and about technology that promises we'll never have to let anyone go.

## The story: two sisters, one mother, a town of robots

You play Pratelle, who comes back to Virtud, the Spanish mountain town where she grew up, after her mother's death. The houses with their clay-tile roofs are where they always were, along with the church, the square and the cafés. But the residents aren't people anymore. They're robots, with heads that look like screens full of code, still living the old routines: watering plants, praying, gossiping outside the market.

The person responsible is Nina, Pratelle's sister. A genius, by every account, who found a way to move people's memories and personalities into mechanical bodies. It all started with their mother's illness. Nina couldn't accept that she was going to die, so she built a way to keep her "alive." To Nina, a body is just a body, and your thoughts are what make you who you are. To Pratelle, death is death. So she comes home with a sledgehammer to break what her sister built.

![Pratelle in a night forest with a giant hammer over her shoulder, facing a church lit in green, with small robots in the background](img/posts/virtue-sledgehammer-forest-church.webp "The road back into town. Screenshot: Deconstructeam / Devolver Digital")

## The hammer: simple, heavy and wildly satisfying

All of the gameplay rests on a single verb: smash. A quick tap shoves whatever's in front of you. Holding a bit longer swings the hammer in a wide arc. Hold it all the way and Pratelle raises it overhead and brings down a blow that takes out an entire wall, sometimes a whole house.

Charging a hit like that takes a few good seconds, and that's deliberate. There's no running, no jumping, no climbing. Pratelle walks slowly, and the hammer is heavy, so every swing feels like a decision. The feedback, especially the sound, is excellent: stone crumbles in a hail of debris, walls collapse with a deafening crash, and knocking a robot into a house just as the roof caves in on it is one of the most satisfying things I've done in a game this year. It's a digital rage room, and it doesn't need to apologize for that.

If you're expecting Teardown-style total destruction, you'll be disappointed. Not everything breaks: cars, trees, mountainsides and even chain-link fences stay put. The game lets you destroy what matters to the story, not the whole world.

![Pratelle mid-swing as chunks of wall and furniture fly out of a stone house](img/posts/virtue-sledgehammer-house-smash.webp "What a fully charged swing looks like. Screenshot: Deconstructeam / Devolver Digital")

## The question a hammer can't answer

This is where Virtue and a Sledgehammer turns from a demolition game into something else. These robots are your neighbors. The gardener, the women praying in church, the group gossiping by the market. Some try to bargain with you. Some beg. Some make a feeble attempt to defend themselves, and some just curl up and shake. A gardener tending his plants isn't an enemy by any normal definition, and the game puts him in front of you and asks: well?

Pratelle never answers them. To her they aren't real, so she doesn't dignify them with a reply. But you, on the other side of the screen, do hear them. And there are moments when it's hard to tell whether what's in front of you is a box reciting memories or something that actually feels.

Then come the other robots. Pratelle is a lesbian and half Arab, and this small town wasn't kind to her growing up. Some robots kept the prejudices of the people they used to be, and they come at her with slurs. The game doesn't soften it. In those moments the swing stops feeling like a philosophical answer to a question about artificial minds and starts feeling personal. And the game is smart enough not to give you the satisfaction you were hoping for: smashing a bigoted robot doesn't undo what the person it imitates once did.

![A long row of white robots with square heads against a red background, with a huge red hammerhead in the foreground](img/posts/virtue-sledgehammer-robot-line.webp "The neighbors are waiting. Screenshot: Deconstructeam / Devolver Digital")

## The memories: what really drives the game

Scattered around town are floating statues in two colors, red and blue. Break enough red ones, or a single blue one, and a short flashback opens up. These scenes are the heart of the game: intimate moments from Pratelle and Nina's childhood and adulthood, from their relationship with their mother, Merche, from the moment illness came into the house, and from the jealousy and resentment that built up between the sisters over the years.

The writing is far leaner than the studio's norm. Deconstructeam is known for very text-heavy games, and here the word count is a fraction of that. That's exactly why every line lands harder. The sisters talk in the sarcastic, coded shorthand of siblings, the kind only someone raised in the same house fully gets, and it takes a while to work out what's really being said.

As in the studio's earlier games, there are dialogue choices. Here, though, they're colder and crueler. More than once I disagreed with every option and picked the least bad one. Which is probably the point: there's no right way to talk to a sister you're furious with and love in the same breath.

![A red-tinted flashback: the two sisters sit on a step, with the line "You're so predictable" above them](img/posts/virtue-sledgehammer-flashback-sisters.webp "The sisters, in flashback. Screenshot: Deconstructeam / Devolver Digital")

## How it looks and sounds

The style mixes 3D models with pixel-art textures, and it has a PlayStation 2-era feel in the best sense. The lighting and color are especially strong: turquoise nights, red sunsets, and wild camera angles that kick in at exactly the right moments. On the other hand, the characters' faces are simple and severe, and the robots look a lot alike, so after a few hours they start to blur together.

The soundtrack by Fingerspit, the studio's regular composer, may be the best thing here. Screaming synths, icy piano slicing through them, and a bassline that slowly falls apart. Some tracks feel like being stuck in the middle of an overcrowded party and never reaching the exit. Combined with the destruction audio, this is a game to play with headphones.

There's also a lot of garbage. Literally. Piles of waste everywhere, from building the robots and from the human bodies that were thrown away. It's hard not to read it as a comment on the environmental cost of the AI industry.

## The AI message, without subtlety

Virtue and a Sledgehammer doesn't try to be balanced about AI. It's against it, loudly, and makes no apologies. A woman in red hurling a hammer at a system that promises eternal life is an image that deliberately echoes Apple's famous "1984" ad. But the game is smarter than a slogan. Its real question isn't whether AI is bad, it's what makes us human, and why knowing we'll all die is part of what gives life meaning. In the end, it argues, letting go of someone you love may be the kindest thing you can do for them.

![Two robots sit on a bench in a garden at night, and one asks "Do you love me?"](img/posts/virtue-sledgehammer-robots-bench.webp "Robots ask hard questions too. Screenshot: Deconstructeam / Devolver Digital")

## Where it stumbles

The big problem is repetition. Over three to five hours, depending on how much you explore, you have two swings, a shove and a handful of enemy types. Combat barely evolves. The opening and the ending ask exactly the same thing of you, and the tougher late-game robots add irritation more than challenge. Somewhere in the last act the destruction stops feeling like release and starts feeling like a chore. You could argue that's the point, that catharsis that never changes becomes a loop you can't escape. It's an interesting argument. It doesn't make the last hour any less tiring.

Navigation is frustrating too. There's no map, no quest markers, just flashing red lights nudging you forward. The town loops around itself, and collecting every flashback means retracing the same paths at Pratelle's slow walking pace. With nothing tracking what you've already found, there's a nagging sense you missed something far behind you. The game is split into chapters, the factory, the main street, the hospital and the graveyard, but in practice it's fairly free-form, which makes it hard to pick back up after a few days away.

There are small physics bugs as well, like chunks of building left hanging in midair after the wall beneath them is gone. Nothing game-breaking, but it pulls you out of the mood for a second.

## For players in Israel

The Steam price in Israel is ₪54.49 (about $17), a bit more than the $14.99 (about ₪48) US players pay. There's no Hebrew: the game supports English, French, Spanish, Japanese, Simplified Chinese, Brazilian Portuguese and Russian. The English isn't hard, but it's full of sarcasm and sibling wordplay, so if you're not comfortable in English you'll miss some of the bite. It runs on Windows only, and the requirements are modest: a GTX 960 or RX 570-class graphics card, 8GB of RAM and 3GB of free space. Controller support is full.

There's also a free demo on Steam, which is a great way to see if the slow pace works for you before paying.

Content warning: racist and homophobic slurs, a parent's illness and death, and moments of graphic violence. Not a game for kids, and not one for an evening when you want something light.

## The bottom line

8/10

Virtue and a Sledgehammer is a short, angry and brave game, with some of the best sound and music work of the year and a family story that isn't afraid to get ugly. The hammer feels fantastic, and the questions it asks about grief, belonging and technology stay with you after the credits. On the other hand, the gameplay repeats itself, the slow walking and map-free navigation wear you down, and the last hour runs long. If you want a fun demolition game, look elsewhere. If you're willing to meet it halfway and bring a bit of yourself along, it's one of the most memorable experiences of 2026.
:::

**What I liked**

- A heavy, satisfying hammer with great destruction audio
- A smart, hard, unsentimental family story
- Lean writing where every line works
- An excellent soundtrack by Fingerspit
- Unforgettable lighting and color
- A free demo and low system requirements

**What I didn't**

- Repetitive gameplay, especially in the final act
- Slow walking and navigation with no map or markers
- Robots that look too much alike
- Small physics bugs
- No Hebrew, and Windows only
