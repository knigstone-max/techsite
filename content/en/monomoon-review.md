Some games explain everything in the first minute. MONOMOON explains nothing. There's no opening text, no dialogue and no arrow telling you where to go. There's a small creature in a blue hat and a red cape, a little crescent moon floating beside it, and a whole world waiting for you to figure out how it works. A few minutes in, you realize that's the whole point.

It came out on Steam on November 11 from Gentle Giant, the studio behind the action roguelite Spiritfall. It's already sitting at Overwhelmingly Positive, with 98% of roughly 900 player reviews in favor, and it isn't hard to see why.

![MONOMOON at a glance: Steam prices in Israel and the US, release date, platform, length, languages and install size](img/posts/monomoon-overview-en.webp "The short version. Graphic: TechSite")

## What kind of game is this?

MONOMOON belongs to a genre with the clunky name "Metroidbrainia." In a regular Metroidvania you upgrade your character with a double jump, a dash or a new weapon, then go back to the doors you couldn't open. Here you never get new abilities. What levels up is **your understanding**. A door that was locked in the first hour opens in the fifth because you worked something out about the world, not because you picked up a new glove. If you've played Animal Well, Tunic or Outer Wilds, you'll recognize the feeling right away.

## The moon

The whole game is built on one idea. You move the creature with the keyboard, like any platformer. You move the moon **with the mouse**, freely, anywhere on screen, including places the creature can't reach. Click, and the moon glows blue. That's where the fun starts.

Objects marked with a moon symbol react to it. Platforms rise and fall, elevators move and switches turn on. Its light reveals dark corners. Creatures react differently. Some are drawn to it and others flee, and some of the best puzzles in the game are built on exactly that. The blue glow also reveals hidden things on the pages of the book you carry, so the moon doubles as a reading tool.

What surprised me most is how much depth Gentle Giant gets out of such a simple mechanic. I won't go further, because half the fun is finding out for yourself what else the moon can do. This is a game you can only really experience once.

![A MONOMOON screenshot: a night forest with a blue tower marked with a moon symbol, the little creature on the left and the moon floating near a platform](img/posts/monomoon-blue-tower.webp "Anything with a moon symbol is a clue. Screenshot: Gentle Giant")

## No combat, very little stress

There are no enemies in the usual sense. You can die from spikes, from something falling on you or from a creature eating you, but checkpoints are very close together, and dying mostly costs a few seconds. Some creatures are genuinely unsettling, like a cyclops with huge hands that prowls dark rooms along the floor and ceiling. Even those are usually part of a puzzle rather than a test of reflexes.

For me this is the smartest decision in the game. In hard Metroidvanias, when you're stuck, the last thing you want is to cross half a map full of enemies just to try another direction. Stuck here? Go somewhere else. The map is bigger than you'd expect and built for non-linear play, so there's almost always something else to try.

## The book

Early on you find an empty book on a desk. As you play you collect feathers, and each one adds a page. The pages vary a lot. Some are almost direct instructions about how creatures behave, some are mysterious diagrams, and some are written in a language of symbols and numbers you have to decode yourself. There's something very Tunic about it, in the best way.

The downside is that because the game is so open, you sometimes find a page **after** you've already worked out what it was trying to teach you. And the reverse happens too. You get stuck on a creature you can't get past, while the page that explains it is on the other side of the map. That's part of the design, but it can be frustrating.

## The world

MONOMOON tells its story without a single word. The world's moon has cracked and its pieces have scattered, and your job is to find them and make it whole again. Everything else you piece together from the scenery, the paintings and the creatures.

The areas are varied: soft green forests, grand temples, dark castle-like corridors and dripping sewers. A forest in the top-right corner of the map has slightly creepy squirrels hiding in the trees and giant acorns marked with moon symbols. It's one of the areas that best blends platforming and puzzles.

![A MONOMOON screenshot: a dark forest with a domed gazebo, glowing eyes inside a tree trunk and the moon floating above the little creature](img/posts/monomoon-forest-gazebo.webp "In the forest, something is always watching. Screenshot: Gentle Giant")

There's a lovely idea in there too. The temple has a floor of blank canvases, and easels are scattered around the world. When you reach one, it paints the view around it, and from then on you can jump from the temple through that painting straight to that spot. If you grew up on Super Mario 64, you'll smile.

![A MONOMOON screenshot: a huge blue temple gate decorated with arches, with the little creature and the moon at the bottom](img/posts/monomoon-temple-door.webp "The temple, where the paintings take you places. Screenshot: Gentle Giant")

## Look and sound

The style is colorful pixel art with thick outlines, and it's simply charming. The main character is one of the cutest heroes of the year. It falls asleep when you leave the controls alone, and it gets dizzy if you spin the moon around it in circles. Water rushes down waterfalls, stars twinkle in the background and the feathers on its head bounce as it walks.

The music opens with a gentle piano tune, and every area has its own theme, from dreamy fairy tale to something a little eerier in the dark zones.

## Controls

The developers recommend keyboard and mouse, and they're right: one hand on the creature, one on the moon. Controllers are supported, with the moon on the right stick, but it feels less natural.

There's a bonus I didn't expect. The game accepts input from several devices at once, so two people can play together on the same couch. One moves the creature with a controller, and the other steers the moon with the mouse. It isn't an official co-op mode, but it works, and it's a great way to play with a partner, or with a kid who can't yet manage two hands doing different things.

## Where it falls short

- **The map.** It marks collectibles, switches you haven't hit and objects you can move, which helps a lot. But you can't zoom in, and areas are shown only as colored outlines that blur together.
- **Lost moments.** Some of the later puzzles are very abstract. The game itself suggests talking it over with friends and trading hints. That's good advice, but if you play completely alone you may stay stuck for a long time.
- **A hazy story.** The wordless storytelling is lovely, but sometimes it's just not clear what happened.

## Price and requirements

On Steam in Israel it costs **₪45.99**, and a 10% launch discount brings it down to ₪41.39 until November 19. In the US it's $11.99, or $10.79 on sale. It's Windows only.

The requirements are tiny: Windows 10, a 2GHz processor, 8GB of RAM, a "relatively modern dedicated graphics card" and just 350MB of disk space. Almost any gaming PC from the last few years will run it.

There's no Hebrew, but there's barely any text either. The interface comes in 16 languages, Arabic included, and you can play most of the game without reading a word.

As for length, the developers say about 10 hours to the end. If you like digging, you'll find the game doesn't stop at the credits, with several layers of secrets for anyone who goes looking.

## The bottom line

8.5/10

MONOMOON takes one idea, a little moon you move with your mouse, and builds a whole world of varied, surprising puzzles around it, with no combat and no hand-holding. It's beautiful, sweet and generous with checkpoints, and at under ₪50 it's a bargain. A confusing map, hints that sometimes arrive too late and a few overly abstract late puzzles keep it from perfection. If you loved Animal Well or Tunic, you should play this.
:::

**What I liked**

- The moon mechanic keeps surprising you
- Varied puzzles, from single rooms to entire areas
- No combat, and checkpoints everywhere
- An open world where there's always another direction to try
- Charming art and music
- Two people can play together on one couch
- Low price and modest requirements

**What I didn't**

- A map with no zoom and areas that blur together
- Hint pages that sometimes show up after you've figured things out
- A few late puzzles that are too abstract
- A story that's sometimes unclear
- Windows only
