Most fantasy games open with a demon army and a prophecy about the hero who'll save the world. Annihilated opens after all of that has already failed. The hero's party went into the demon king's dungeon and vanished. A rescue team went after them, and nearly all of it died on the way down. You're the last survivor, a soldier with no fancy name, and when you finally reach the heroes, they're sitting around playing cards as if nothing happened.

Then it turns out each of them has lost something. The Hero can only say two words: "Yes" and "No." The Mage has lost her magic. The Warrior has lost his body, and his soul now lives in an adorable doll, which he seems thrilled about. And the Priest has lost something the game hides behind a black bar at first, and in the meantime has started believing in something called the Great One. One more problem: you can only carry one of them out.

This is Annihilated, the debut from small Japanese studio Death Kumiai, out this week on Steam for Windows. It's a roguelite with card battles, but at heart it's a dark, funny and sad visual novel where the fights are how you get to the next chapter.

![Annihilated at a glance: Steam price in dollars and shekels, four routes of about six hours, PC only, languages and content warning](img/posts/annihilated-overview-en.webp "The short version. Graphic: TechSite")

## The first choice is the hardest

Every playthrough starts with picking who comes with you. Each of the four heroes has a full route of their own, around six hours long, with several endings. The dungeon layout is similar between routes, but the story, the dialogue, the secrets and even some of the enemies change completely. It's not "the same game with a different skin." Each route tells what happened to the party from the point of view of whoever is walking beside you, and putting all four together gives you the whole picture.

The writing is the heart of the game, and there's a lot of it: more than 200,000 words. The doll Warrior is the most fun member of the group. He's absurdly upbeat and asks every few minutes whether he isn't super cute. But under the jokes is someone born into the role of fighter who never really chose it, and he has unfinished business with one of the dungeon's lords, the Villainess, who used to be his fiancée. The Hero, armed with only "yes" and "no," somehow manages full conversations, and it's one of the cleverest tricks in the game. The Priest was the route I most wanted to open, precisely because of that black bar.

![The Warrior, whose soul lives in a doll, talking to the Soldier in the dungeon](img/posts/annihilated-warrior-doll.webp "The Warrior in his new body. Image: Death Kumiai / Flashlight Games")

The Soldier, the character you play, is not a silent protagonist. The Soldier has more lines than anyone, mostly inner monologue about the heroes, the enemies and the Soldier's own place in all this. Most of the time it works. Sometimes it runs too long and spells out what the scene already showed. There were moments I just wanted to move on.

Fair warning: this game is dark. There's blood, death, psychological horror and twisted illustrations next to the cute characters. The Steam page says so plainly, and adds, with some humor, that if you expect too much of these elements you may be disappointed. It's not a horror game, but it's not for kids either.

## Combat: every weapon is a card, and every card runs out

Between story scenes there's the dungeon. Each floor is a map of connected nodes, Slay the Spire style: you pick a path, and each node holds a fight, a random event, a treasure chest or a campfire. Each path ends with a story-related mini-boss.

In battle, every weapon and item you carry is a card, and every card has a limited number of uses. Enemies show you what they'll do next turn, so there's room to plan. The hero you picked fights beside you, and each one has a role: the Hero can't really die, and the Priest heals like nobody else.

![A card battle: a row of colorful weapon cards at the bottom of the screen, each showing its remaining uses, with enemies above](img/posts/annihilated-card-battle.webp "Every weapon is a card with limited uses. Image: Death Kumiai / Flashlight Games")

The best idea in the game is that a weapon is strongest on its last use, right before it breaks. Some cards double their effect when destroyed, so a Potion, for example, heals twice as much. And if you stagger an enemy and then land a weapon's final hit, you get a Fatal Strike: the enemy dies instantly and you take its gear. That's how most of your arsenal comes together. You rarely hold on to the same weapon for long, and every steal hands you a new card to try.

You can also break a weapon on purpose, even with uses left. That's freeing. There's no point saving items "for the right moment," because the right moment is usually now.

## Curses: the currency that ties it together

Every item that breaks leaves behind a Curse. It's the main currency, and it has three uses:

- **Defy:** rolled a terrible result? Pay a Curse and it never happened.
- **Forge:** once you're far enough in, you can turn Curses into items you're missing.
- **Grow:** the rest becomes experience, weapon upgrades and better healing at campfires.

At campfires you rest, upgrade and save. The game also saves at the start of each stage. And when you die, you don't go back to the beginning like a typical roguelite. You go back to the start of the floor where you fell, after reassigning some stats or forging items. Every death leaves you a little stronger. If you love hardcore roguelites, you'll find this too forgiving. If you're here for the story, and I think that's most of this game's audience, you'll be grateful.

## Timing or luck: two battle styles

Before you start, you choose a battle style. With timing, every strike asks you to click at the right moment as a cursor sweeps over a hit zone. Stronger weapons get faster cursors or narrower zones, and heavy weapons demand several quick clicks in a row. When there are several enemies on screen, you can time a hit to catch a few of them at once, and that's when combat really comes alive.

With probability, the timing gauge disappears and outcomes are left to the dice. Both styles reach the ending, and that's the right call. People who are here for the characters shouldn't get stuck on a fight because of their reflexes.

![A giant roulette wheel called Dead or Alive with success and failure slices and a red START button, inside a dungeon room](img/posts/annihilated-dead-or-alive.webp "The Dead or Alive wheel: plenty of events come down to this. Image: Death Kumiai / Flashlight Games")

## Where it falls short

Three problems keep coming back.

**The combat is shallower than it looks.** Weapon breaking is brilliant on paper, but often the winning strategy is simple: hit with your strongest weapon, save its last swing for the toughest enemy, repeat. It's fun, but after a route or two you can feel the pattern.

**The map hides too much.** There isn't enough information about what waits at each node, so route planning is often guesswork. And many random events boil down to a roulette wheel that checks a single stat. Some are very funny, like a lone goblin waving a white flag, but more variety would help.

**The English translation is rough.** There are spelling and grammar mistakes here and there. It never gets in the way of understanding, and sometimes it even adds charm, but if English is your second language, know that there's a lot of text and it isn't always polished. There's no Hebrew, and this is a game that's almost all reading.

One smaller point: in big fights with lots of enemies, the interface gets crowded and the information blurs together.

## How it looks and sounds

There are more than 400 hand-drawn illustrations, in a deliberately rough style that swings without apology from warm campfire moments to blood-spattered portraits. Key scenes play out like a run of manga pages, and they stay with you after you close the game. The contrast between the cute characters and what happens to them is exactly what gives the dark moments their weight.

The soundtrack, 27 tracks by composer Yuji Ninomiya, is an open tribute to '90s JRPGs: driving battle music and haunting dungeon themes. It's also sold separately on Steam, and if you grew up on games from that era, you'll want it.

## Performance and price

The requirements are modest: Windows 11, a Ryzen 5 5625U or equivalent, 8GB of RAM, integrated graphics and 2GB of space. Any reasonable laptop will run it. There's no console or Mac version for now.

On Steam in Israel it's ₪60.99 ($16.99), and until November 23 there's a 10% launch discount, bringing it to ₪54.89 ($15.29). For four routes of about six hours each, that's a very fair price. And for streamers and video makers: the developers explicitly allow streaming and videos, monetization included.

## The bottom line

7/10

Annihilated is a game with an original idea and a big heart. Four broken heroes, four routes that are each a complete story, writing that manages to be funny and cruel in the same scene, and art and music that don't look like they came from a small studio. The weapon-breaking system is brilliant in concept and shallower in practice, the map and roulettes repeat themselves, and the translation and long dialogue ask for patience. If you're here for the story and the characters, it's one of this fall's nicest surprises. If you want a challenging card roguelite, there are deeper options.
:::

**What I liked**

- Four full routes with different endings and strong writing
- Weapons that peak right before they break, and Fatal Strikes that steal gear
- Two battle styles, so anyone can reach the end
- 400+ illustrations and a '90s-inspired soundtrack
- A low price for this much content

**What I didn't**

- Combat that thins out after a few hours
- A map without enough information, and too many roulettes
- Dialogue that sometimes runs too long
- A rough translation, and no Hebrew
- PC only
