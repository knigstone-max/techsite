Every winter the same question comes back: switch the AC to heat, or drag the radiator out of storage? Next week it becomes a money question. From December 1, anyone on Israel's time-of-use tariff pays almost 2.5 times as much between 17:00 and 22:00 as the rest of the day, which is exactly when everyone's home and the heating goes on. We covered that in our [winter tariff guide](/techsite/en/posts/winter-tou-electricity-peak-hours-appliances/).

So instead of talking in general terms, we picked three real devices you can buy today, checked how much power each draws according to its spec sheet, and did the maths.

## The three contenders

**Inverter AC: Electra Titanium INV 140.** A 1 HP wall-mounted unit rated A++. According to Electra's spec sheet, in heating mode it puts out 8,530 BTU, roughly 2.5 kW of heat, while drawing 660 W. Electra's site lists it at **₪1,830 (about $547)** including delivery. If there's no AC in the room yet, check what installation will cost too.

**Oil radiator: Fujicom FJ-RD9-2000.** Nine fins, 2,000 W, a thermostat, three heat settings and a Shabbat timer. **₪189 (about $57)** at KSP, or ₪160 (about $48) in Eilat.

**Bathroom fan heater: Electra HBH-2007B.** Wall-mounted, with a towel rail, 1,000 W and 2,000 W settings, a weekly timer and a thermostat from 10 to 49°C. Machsanei Hashmal has it for **₪159 (about $48)**, down from ₪219 (about $65).

## What an hour costs

![Comparison table: Electra AC, Fujicom radiator and Electra fan heater, with price, power draw, heat output and hourly cost on the flat rate, winter peak and winter off-peak](img/posts/heating-compare-table-en.webp "Cost per hour at full power. Graphic: TechSite")

The maths is simple: power times the price of a kWh. Per the tariff book, a regular home pays 63.52 agorot per kWh on the flat rate. On time-of-use in winter it's 114.70 agorot at peak and 46.63 off-peak, VAT included.

- **The AC:** 42 agorot an hour on the flat rate (about 13 cents). 76 agorot at peak (about 23 cents). 31 agorot off-peak (about 9 cents).
- **The radiator, and the fan heater at 2,000 W:** ₪1.27 an hour on the flat rate (about 38 cents). ₪2.29 at peak (about 69 cents). 93 agorot off-peak (about 28 cents).
- **The fan heater at 1,000 W:** half that, 64 agorot an hour on the flat rate (about 19 cents).

Now look at the heat column. The AC puts out more heat, 2.5 kW, from a third of the power.

## Why the AC is so much cheaper

A radiator and a fan heater are resistive heaters. Every watt that goes in comes out as a watt of heat, and no more. 2,000 W from the socket gives you 2 kW of heat.

An AC doesn't make heat, it moves it. There's heat in the outdoor air even when it's cold, and the compressor pulls it in. Going by the two figures on Electra's spec sheet, every kilowatt of electricity brings in about 3.8 kW of heat. Electra itself lists a heating COP of 5.2, probably measured under different conditions. Either way it's several times better than any resistive heater.

There's a limit. The colder it is outside, the harder the AC works and the less it delivers. On the coastal plain you'll barely notice. In Jerusalem, the Upper Galilee or on a cold Negev night efficiency drops, but it still stays well ahead of a radiator.

## A whole winter in numbers

![Chart: three hours of heat every evening over 90 winter days, radiator vs AC, at peak and on the flat rate](img/posts/heating-winter-cost-en.webp "One winter, added up. Graphic: TechSite")

Say three hours of heating every evening, in the peak window, from December to the end of February. That's 90 evenings.

- **A 2,000 W radiator or fan heater:** about ₪619 ($185).
- **The AC:** about ₪204 ($61).
- **The gap:** about ₪415 ($124) in one winter.

On the flat rate the gap is smaller, about ₪230 ($69), but still clear.

One caveat: this assumes full power the whole time. In practice, once the room reaches temperature the thermostat throttles back or switches off, so real bills will be lower for all three. What matters here is the ratio between them.

## So when does a radiator or fan heater make sense?

The numbers say AC, but there are cases where that doesn't work:

- **There's no AC in the room.** The AC costs ₪1,641 (about $491) more than the radiator, before installation. If you're buying it only for winter, by our numbers it takes several winters to earn that back. If you were planning an AC for the summer anyway, that's a different calculation.
- **The bathroom.** That's what Electra's fan heater is built for: wall-mounted, IP23-rated, with a towel rail. Fifteen minutes before a shower on a timer is the cheapest line in the whole table.
- **Heating one corner for a short time.** A fan heater at 1,000 W next to your desk for an hour is 64 agorot. No point heating the whole living room for that.
- **People who can't stand the AC's airflow.** An oil radiator works with no fan and no noise, and its fins stay warm for a while after you switch it off. Some people simply sleep better that way.

## Tips that save money with all three

- **Heat before 17:00.** On time-of-use, switch on half an hour before the peak, then turn it down.
- **Close doors.** All of these heat a room, not a flat.
- **With the AC:** 20 to 22°C is enough. Clean the filters before winter; a clogged filter cuts efficiency.
- **With the radiator and the fan heater:** plug straight into a wall socket, not a power strip. Don't cover them or dry laundry on them, and keep them away from curtains.

## The bottom line

If there's an inverter AC in the room, it's the cheapest way to heat it, by a wide margin. Radiators and fan heaters cost roughly three times as much per hour, but they're cheap to buy, quieter (the radiator) or made for the bathroom (the fan heater). If you're on time-of-use, think twice before switching on a 2,000 W heater at seven in the evening.
