Every phone camera, from a budget Redmi to an iPhone, throws away a large part of the light it collects before a single photo is taken. An Israeli startup called PxE Imaging thinks that's unnecessary, and yesterday it was acquired by a company that knows how to get optics into phones at scale.

The buyer is Metalenz, a Boston company spun out of Harvard in 2016. Its speciality is metasurface optics: ultra-thin lens structures made with chip manufacturing processes and patterned directly onto image sensors. The company says it has shipped more than 300 million of them in consumer devices, and its Polar ID face-unlock system went into mass production last year. The terms of the deal weren't disclosed.

## What's wrong with today's cameras

To capture colour, nearly every digital camera puts a Bayer filter over its sensor: a mosaic of tiny red, green and blue squares, one per pixel. Each pixel only "sees" one colour, and software fills in the rest. The catch, according to Metalenz, is that the filter absorbs about 60% of the incoming light. That light is simply gone.

This is the reason phone makers invest so heavily in night modes, multi-frame processing and AI. Metalenz CEO Rob Devlin told Wired that Apple, Samsung and Google spend hundreds of millions on software to improve their cameras, but once the light has been absorbed, no amount of software can bring it back.

![Two diagrams side by side: a Bayer filter that absorbs part of the light, and PxE's Holocoder layer that splits light by colour onto the sensor](img/posts/holocoder-vs-bayer-en.webp "Today's colour filter versus PxE's approach. Graphic: TechSite")

## What PxE built

PxE's technology is called Holocoder, and it was invented by the company's co-founder and CEO, Yoav Berlatzky. It's a layer patterned straight onto a standard sensor, like Metalenz's own products, and it uses the wave nature of light to sort it by wavelength instead of filtering it out. According to Metalenz, that replaces the Bayer filter and delivers 2.5 times more light to the sensor, which should help in both dark and very bright scenes.

There's a second benefit. The same layer also encodes depth information, so a single camera can capture a colour image and a depth map at the same time. Today that usually needs an extra sensor, an extra camera or a lot of processing. Metalenz says that decoding this in hardware cuts the computing load, which matters for AI that runs on the device itself, in phones, cars, drones and robots.

## When will you see it

Not soon. PxE's technology isn't on the market yet. Devlin told Wired it could reach products as early as the end of 2027, and Dealroom notes that consumer devices aren't expected before late 2027 or early 2028. For now, Metalenz can't combine Holocoder with its existing optics either, though that's the long-term plan.

So nobody in Israel will be buying a phone with Holocoder this year or next. Still, it's another case of an Israeli imaging startup ending up inside a global supply chain, and if the 2.5x figure holds up in real products, it would be one of the bigger hardware jumps phone cameras have seen in years.
