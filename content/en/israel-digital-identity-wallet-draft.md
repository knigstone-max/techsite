Think about how many times you've sent a photo of your ID card in recent years. To your mobile carrier, your insurance agent, your bank, some website you signed up for once. Each of those photos now sits in a database somewhere, and any database can leak. The state wants to change that.

On Monday, during National Cyber Defense Week, Israel's National Cyber Directorate and National Digital Agency published a draft national digital identity strategy. It was put together with the Population and Immigration Authority, the Finance Ministry and the Bank of Israel, and it's open for public comment until November 30, 2026.

## A national digital wallet

At the heart of the plan is an identity wallet on your phone, built by the National Digital Agency. It's meant to hold official documents and certificates, starting with your ID card and driving licence, and let you show them straight from the screen.

The key part is legal status. Gadgety reports that the state is pushing amendments that would give the digital card full equivalence with the physical one, including when a police officer or clerk asks for it in person. Until that legislation passes, it's an intention, not a fact.

The wallet is supposed to follow a principle called selective disclosure: you share only what's needed. If you have to prove you're over a certain age, you can do that without revealing your address, ID number or parents' names.

## One login, beyond government

The second move is expanding the national login system, the one Israelis already use for government websites. About 5.5 million residents are registered. Under the draft, it would gradually extend to private bodies such as banks, insurers and health organizations, so a single login replaces a separate ID check at each one.

According to CyberNow, the Directorate promises that your accounts with private companies stay separate, that the system is only used to verify identity, and that the government won't see what you do with those companies. Those are statements about a draft, and there's no way to check them yet. No rollout timeline has been published.

## Why now: AI and fakes

The draft is upfront about the reason. Generative AI has made forged documents, deepfake videos and synthetic identities cheap and easy. So the plan includes a national monitoring capability to spot anomalies across the life of a digital identity. It also calls for early preparation for encryption that can withstand quantum computers, so signatures and checks made today can't be broken a few years from now.

## What it means for you

The upside is clear: fewer ID photos floating around in databases, and less paperwork. There's a flip side, though. When one account opens your bank, insurance and health records, it becomes the most valuable target for attackers. Protecting it stops being a matter of convenience.

A few things you can do now:

- **If you have a national login account,** give it a unique password you don't use anywhere else, and turn on two-step verification if it's offered.
- **When someone asks for a photo of your ID,** ask why. Sometimes the number or another check is enough, and sending it over WhatsApp or email leaves a copy in at least two more places.
- **A link asking for identity details** can be checked before you click with the Cyber Directorate's Click Check service.
- **If you have views on the wallet,** the draft is on the National Cyber Directorate's website, and comments are open until November 30.
