There's a whole genre of horse games built for eight-year-olds: a shiny horse, a "groom" button and stars raining from the sky. Horses of Hoofprint Bay is the opposite. It looks like an illustrated children's book, but under the drawings is a management game that treats a horse like an animal: it needs to eat, drink and rest, and it can get sick if you neglect it.

It came out on Steam on November 4 from **Thogli Studios**, a team of three developers who call themselves horse girls, published by The Mane Quest. The store page also says it was made entirely by humans, without generative AI. You can tell in every frame.

![Horses of Hoofprint Bay at a glance: release date, Steam price in Israel and the US, Steam Deck status, and what you do in the game](img/posts/horses-of-hoofprint-bay-overview-en.webp "The short version. Graphic: TechSite")

## What it's about

There's no story, and that's on purpose. You arrive by ferry at a seaside town, having saved enough to buy a plot of land with a crooked shed that passes for a stable and an old trailer to live in. You pick a first mare from a lineup, ride her home, and from there it's up to you.

No town to save, no villain, no family legacy to restore. Instead of a plot there's a list of goals: move in, feed and water, build an arena, win your first ribbon. Each one pays money and prestige and unlocks the next. If you want characters and dialogue, they aren't here. If you like watching something grow slowly, you'll fall for it.

## The morning routine

Every in-game day starts with chores, and the whole game is built around them. You drag hay to the rack, haul water to the trough, muck out the stall and groom your horse. There are four tools: a curry comb that lifts dirt, a soft brush that smooths the coat, a hoof pick and a brush for the mane and tail. You only clean one hoof, a smart call that keeps grooming from turning into busywork.

At the end of the day you press "New Day" and get a summary of your money, prestige and how your horses changed.

It sounds repetitive, and it is. But there's a reason. The game ties every system together: feed affects weight, weight affects health, training affects the horse's confidence, and confidence affects every ride. Skip the hoof pick and you'll be treating an abscess. A dirty stall can lead to thrush. The list of ailments is long and specific, including lameness, laminitis and tendon injuries, each with a cause and a recovery time. You treat small problems yourself with antiseptic or cream, and call the vet for big ones. And if a horse gets dehydrated or malnourished and nobody steps in, animal welfare takes it away.

I like this approach. In most horse games one bandage fixes everything. Here, chores matter because they have consequences.

## The library in the trailer

What surprised me most is the library. As you progress, books unlock on horse care, injuries, training, gaits, competitions, confidence, breeding and traits. They go into real detail, down to the footfall pattern of the walk, trot, canter and gallop. The developers say not everything is fully realistic, but they aim for accuracy wherever it doesn't hurt the fun. If you've never stood next to a horse, it's a gentle little lesson. If you ride, it's just nice to see someone who did their homework.

## Training and riding

Training is the closest thing to action here. It plays out on short side-on courses with a strength bar at the top. When strength runs out, the horse refuses jumps and the session fails, so you switch gaits: trot is the safe choice early on, canter suits high jumps and gallop handles wide obstacles like water ditches.

There's plenty of variety: cavaletti for beginners, Caprilli exercises where you hold a gait and change it at the right marker, show jumping, cross-country and lunging. The horse doesn't always cooperate either. A small icon warns that it's about to slow down or rush off, and you have a moment to correct it. It gives the horse a sense of having a mind of its own.

Confidence training is a lovely little puzzle: the horse spots a plastic bag or an umbrella, and you have to read its body language. Head low and nose forward means curious, so you praise it. Head up, ears back and a tense face means scared, so you back off. Push too hard and it bolts, and the bolting animation is funny even when it costs you.

![Screenshot from Horses of Hoofprint Bay: a show jumping competition, a rider on a grey horse approaching a fence, with white tents, parasols and spectators behind](img/posts/horses-of-hoofprint-bay-showjumping.webp "A show jumping round. Screenshot: Thogli Studios")

## Lessons, boarders and competitions

Money comes from several directions. **Riding lessons** are a separate minigame: a student sits on your horse and you fix their posture as it slips, with keys for the seat, legs and hand height. Lessons pay well, but each one tires the horse.

**Boarding** is another income stream: owners leave a horse with you for a while at one of three levels, from feed only up to training toward a target before the stay ends. The top level pays the most, if you can pull it off.

**Competitions** can be entered once a day, and one horse can enter several classes if it qualifies. You need a licence, a trailer and a clean horse. The horse shows up with a braided mane, there's an announcer, and it feels like a real show. Prestige rises when you win and care for horses well, and drops when you fail, ride without a helmet or turn up with a dirty horse.

## Money runs out fast

This is where the game is less forgiving. Every building, from the arena to the breeding station, goes up overnight but costs money every day. Your balance can dip below zero very quickly, especially early on. Build too fast and you'll get stuck.

Horses have limits too. Each one has a potential, and once it maxes out you have to decide: sell, buy a better horse or breed one. Selling your first mare is a sad moment, but that's the business.

## Breeding

Breeding is the long game. A foal inherits body colour and mane colour separately, and its potential usually lands a little above the parents' average. Related parents lower it, so pair unrelated horses. Pregnancy lasts eight in-game days, and some personality traits are inherited while others are random: mud lover, neat freak, rebellious, good mom. A "bad inheritance" trait can ruin a whole pairing, and finding out on the day the foal is born is annoying and a little funny.

## Accessibility and difficulty

One of the things it does best. You can turn on auto-jumping so timing never blocks you, or show cones before each fence to tell you when to press. Sliders cover goal rewards, prestige and competition difficulty, and you can change them mid-save. Going through a stressful stretch? Turn it down and keep playing, no restart needed.

## Looks and sound

Everything is hand-drawn, nostalgic without feeling dated. The farm has a lighthouse on the horizon, chickens and a napping dog; the forest trails have mushrooms on logs and apple trees. Horses show their mood through small animations, like two of them grooming each other, and the stable visibly changes as you upgrade it, from a crooked shed to a proper barn.

The music shifts when you head to town or out on a trail, and the effects do their job: snorts, whinnies, hay being tossed, crickets at night. One weak spot: the canter and gallop sound hollow, as if you're riding in a hall rather than on dirt.

## What works less well

- **Caprilli timing** isn't clear at first, and it takes a few tries to learn exactly when to press
- **Some things go unexplained**: hovering over a trait shows nothing, and the star icon has no tooltip
- **One careless click** at the breeder can start a pregnancy you didn't plan
- **The loop repeats** in the later stages, and it's a grind, unapologetically so

## For Israeli players

It costs **₪98.99 on Steam** (about $29.76), versus **$26.99** in the US. It runs on Windows and Linux and is rated Playable on Steam Deck. There's no Hebrew, but there are 24 languages and the English is simple. There's also a free demo, worth starting with if you're not sure the routine is for you. On Steam it's rated Overwhelmingly Positive, with 98% positive reviews.

## The bottom line

8.5/10

Horses of Hoofprint Bay is a hand-drawn stable sim that takes horses seriously: daily care with consequences, training where the horse has a personality, competitions, breeding and a library that teaches real things. It's repetitive, money is tight early on and a few things go unexplained, but if you enjoy a calm routine and games that reward patience, you'll keep coming back for one more day.
:::

**What I liked**

- Warm, detailed hand-drawn art
- Systems that connect: feed, health, training and confidence
- A library that teaches about real horses
- Confidence training and horses with personality
- Accessibility options and difficulty you can change mid-save

**What I liked less**

- Very repetitive later on
- Tight money early on
- Unclear timing in some exercises
- Missing explanations for traits and icons
- No Hebrew
