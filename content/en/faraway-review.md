Some games explain themselves in thirty seconds and then take your whole evening. Faraway is one of them. You are a shooting star flying in a straight line through space. Hold the one button near a star and you latch on and start orbiting it. Let go and you fly off in whatever direction you were facing at that moment. That is it. No other buttons, no skill menu, no upgrade tree.

Behind that simplicity sit 16 years of work by a single developer, Steph Thirion, who released the acclaimed iPhone puzzler Eliss back in 2009. Faraway launched on November 5 from Annapurna Interactive on Switch, Switch 2, PS5, Xbox Series X|S and PC. On the Israeli PlayStation Store it is ₪40 (about $13), with a launch discount to ₪32 through November 19. In the US it is $9.99.

![Faraway at a glance: ₪40 on the Israeli PS Store, ₪32 at launch, and the three game modes](img/posts/faraway-overview-en.webp "What ₪40 gets you. Graphic: TechSite")

## How it plays

The heart of Faraway is round zones in space packed with stars. Once you enter one, the goal is to link as many stars as possible with lines, one after another, without crossing a line you already drew. Every segment scores. Close a loop, returning to a linked star to complete a shape, and you get a multiplier. The more intricate the constellation, the bigger the jump in points.

The catch is that you have no direct steering. You do not pick where to fly, you pick when to let go. With several stars close together, it is not always clear which one you will latch onto. Early on that is frustrating. After a few runs the timing starts to click: sometimes you circle slowly and line up the shot, sometimes you fling yourself across the zone at speed.

![The shooting star orbits a star inside a dark zone on a red background, with white lines linking stars](img/posts/faraway-orbit.webp "Orbit, release, connect. Image: Annapurna Interactive")

## Racing a fading light

Outside the zones, your star is burning out. Its energy drains constantly, and when it hits zero you get a few seconds to reach the nearest zone and score enough to earn time back. Every constellation you complete extends your life. When the light goes out, you start over. That structure, one continuous run you try to stretch as far as possible, gives Faraway the feel of an old arcade cabinet. To move up a level, you need one run that is good enough.

Space is randomly generated, so no two runs are alike. The main mode has seven levels, and as your score climbs, larger zones open up that allow more complex constellations.

## Echoes and Ascents: a break from the clock

As you progress, two separate challenge types unlock. Echoes are calmer missions where you form a set number of constellations or trace particular shapes. They work like a practice range and teach maneuvers that are hard to discover under time pressure. Ascents are focused score challenges with targets that keep rising.

I like that split. When the main mode starts to grate, Echoes give you a reason to keep playing without the clock on your neck, and you come back to the runs with a new trick.

![A pink-orange comet trail inside a dark zone on a cyan background, with lines linking stars](img/posts/faraway-blue-zone.webp "Each zone gets its own color. Image: Annapurna Interactive")

## Look and sound

Faraway looks like a minimalist poster in motion: bold background colors, dark circles, clean lines and a glowing trail. There are no particle storms or blinding effects, and that is exactly why it feels so pleasant. The music is soft and melodic and follows the motion without taking over. It is a game that works best with headphones, quietly, after a long day.

It opens with a "dream", a short tutorial covering the basics, then leaves you alone. There is very little text, and what there is comes in several languages, though not Hebrew. You really do not need it here.

## What worked less well

The controls are the game's biggest strength and also its weakness. In tight clusters your star sometimes latches onto the wrong one, and a carefully built constellation breaks because one line crossed another. That is part of the challenge, but at times it feels unfair.

The pacing will not suit everyone either. Between zones there are stretches of flying through empty space, and if you only want to build constellations you will find yourself waiting. And if you want dozens of hours of content with a story and characters, this is not it. Faraway is a fifteen-minutes-here, fifteen-minutes-there game.

## Who it is for

If you loved Eliss, Tetris Effect or minimalist arcade games in general, there is something special here. At ₪40, and certainly at ₪32 during the launch discount, it is one of November's cheapest and most original releases. It is a particularly good fit for Switch and Switch 2 in handheld mode, for short sessions on the go.

## Bottom line

8/10

Faraway is a smart, beautiful and calming arcade game that builds real depth out of a single button. The run structure, loop multipliers and separate challenges keep pulling you back. It loses points for controls that can feel imprecise among crowded stars and for empty flight between zones. For the price of a small meal, it is hard to find a game with more idea per shekel.
:::

**What I liked**

- One button, with depth that reveals itself slowly
- Loop multipliers that reward planning
- Echoes and Ascents that teach without pressure
- Clean visuals and gentle music
- Low price: ₪40, or ₪32 through Nov 19

**What I liked less**

- The star sometimes latches onto the wrong target
- Empty flight stretches between zones
- Not much content if you want a long game
- No Hebrew in the menus
