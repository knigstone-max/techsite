Some games sell themselves in one sentence. For Castlebound, that sentence is: your castle walks. It wakes up, sprouts a pair of stubby legs and starts marching across the map, while skeletons, bats, ghosts and monsters pour in from every direction. Your job, alone or with up to three friends on the same couch, is to keep it alive until the next stop.

The game comes from Ogre Pixel, a small Mexican studio that previously made Lonesome Village and A Tiny Sticker Tale. Castlebound launched on November 11 on Steam, as well as Switch and Switch 2, following a Kickstarter campaign, and the studio is publishing it itself. The result is one of the cutest games of the month, with the heart of a '90s arcade game and a few design decisions that keep it from being the family game it looks like.

![Castlebound at a glance: Steam price in shekels and dollars, 1 to 4 players, platforms, four heroes, four regions and languages](img/posts/castlebound-overview-en.webp "The short version. Graphic: TechSite")

## The story: an archer, her grandfather, and a castle that wakes up

The plot is simple and sweet, and rightly doesn't try to be more than that. Lenora, a young archer, and her grandfather Vargus, a veteran wizard, awaken an ancient structure that turns into a living fortress. A mysterious fairy named Coronya guides them, and they set out to recruit more fighters to push back the evil forces threatening the kingdom. There's a fairly long opening cutscene, and then the game gets straight to the point: jump, shoot, and yell at your friends to get back to the gate.

Between runs you return to the castle itself, which serves as your base. Inside it looks like a dollhouse, with bookshelves, a giant cauldron and bunk beds on the upper floor. It's a small touch, but it makes the place feel like a home rather than just a menu.

## How it plays

The castle always hugs the left side of the screen while the view scrolls forward. Around it float wooden platforms, and enemies come from all sides trying to reach the gate. The castle has its own health bar, and so do you. So you're constantly making the call: jump up and take out the bat diving at you, or the skeleton already gnawing at the door.

The archer, your first hero, can jump, fire arrows and do a short backward dash. Most of the time the combat feels like a classic shooter: tap or hold fire, hop between platforms, and try to clear the screen before anything hits the castle. It's fast, loud and very moreish, especially once the screen fills up with upgrade effects.

![Four heroes marked P1 to P4 fighting skeletons and ghosts by the castle gate on a blue night](img/posts/castlebound-four-players.webp "Four players on one screen. Screenshot: Ogre Pixel")

## Roguelite: die, collect coins, come back stronger

At the end of every run, win or lose, you earn Soul Coins. You spend them on permanent upgrades: faster shooting, more health, higher movement speed. Between stage segments you also get upgrade cards with hero-specific boosts, like explosive arrows for the archer or extra familiars for the wizard. Most of those cards have to be unlocked in the shop first, again with coins.

![The Hero Cards shop: a row of unlocked and locked cards, with a Big slash card and a buy button beside them](img/posts/castlebound-hero-cards.webp "The card shop in the castle. Screenshot: Ogre Pixel")

It works, but it's also where Castlebound drifts furthest from its arcade roots. For the first few hours the heroes feel weak, and repeated attempts at the first region can turn into a grind. You do feel progress with every run, which matters, but anyone expecting a game you just pick up and beat, like the classics it evokes, will need some patience.

Between stages you pick a route. The "Choose your path" screen offers two options, each with its own challenge and reward, so every run branches a little differently. It's a nice idea that adds a small, interesting decision at each stop.

![Path selection screen: two options, one with undead enemies and a coin reward, the other with forest creatures and a health reward](img/posts/castlebound-choose-path.webp "Every stop, you pick a road. Screenshot: Ogre Pixel")

## Four heroes, and one steals the show

There are four heroes, and anyone can play any of them, even several players as the same hero. The archer is available from the start, the wizard unlocks soon after, the slinger costs 300 Soul Coins, and the knight, Galahad, only unlocks once you're halfway through the desert.

The first three play fairly alike: nonstop shooting with different projectiles. The wizard stands above the rest. His shots pass through obstacles, and his secondary ability, a familiar that behaves like a mine, moving with the screen and exploding when an enemy touches it, is simply stronger than anything the others have. The other heroes' secondary abilities are defensive, and in a game like this, more damage is just better. I'd rather see this as a balance issue that a patch will fix, because right now there's one "correct" pick.

The knight is the most interesting hero, because he's the only one who fights up close. He has to get near enemies, and his upgrades revolve around defense and reflecting damage. He's also a great choice for younger players, who find it easier to press a button when the enemy is right in front of them. The catch: he only unlocks midway through the second region, so you'll put in a fair amount of play before you get him.

## Couch co-op: where it should shine

Castlebound is built first and foremost as a local multiplayer game. Up to four people share one screen, each hero gets a colored P1 to P4 marker, and every character has a thick white outline, so even when the screen is packed with skeletons and a giant boss, you can always find yourself. Every extra player needs their own controller. If your friends aren't in the same room, Steam's Remote Play Together streams the game to them online.

The problem is difficulty. Enemies and their health scale with the number of players, which makes sense on paper. In practice, co-op bosses get absurd amounts of health even after plenty of upgrades, and the fights against them are long and built from several stages that repeat themselves. The first boss in particular is a sharp difficulty spike. On a family night with kids, that's exactly the moment somebody puts the controller down and walks off.

![A giant purple boss sprawled across a stone hall, facing small heroes shooting at it](img/posts/castlebound-boss.webp "The bosses are huge, and in co-op very durable. Screenshot: Ogre Pixel")

## Regions and modes

The main journey runs through four regions, a forest, a desert, a graveyard and a mountain, and ends with a fight against a final boss. Each region also has a Hard version if you want more challenge. Each has its own enemies, and the graveyard is the best designed: zombies, hands bursting out of the ground, flying specters and fast ravens. Some stages also scroll in other directions, a refreshing change that adds challenge.

Then there's Arcade mode, which may be the nicest surprise here. It's endless scrolling with more and more enemies and upgrades, and it's simply fun. It's also practical: a good Arcade run can bring in plenty of Soul Coins and cut down the grind in the main game.

## Art and music

This is where Castlebound is strongest. Everything looks like paper cutouts standing inside a diorama: flat, hand-drawn characters with thick white borders against soft 3D sets of round trees, glowing moons and stone walls. The palette moves between turquoise forests, purple nights and orange torchlight, so even a crowded fight feels warm. If you loved the look of Paper Mario, you'll feel right at home. And the retro-inspired music is very catchy.

![Night battle by the castle: heroes fire a long blue laser through a swarm of enemies under a full moon](img/posts/castlebound-night-laser.webp "When the upgrades start stacking up. Screenshot: Ogre Pixel")

## Price, platforms and what to know

On Steam in Israel, Castlebound costs ₪35.99 (about $11.25), and with the 15% launch discount it drops to ₪30.59 (about $9.56). In the US it's $9.99 (about ₪32), or $8.49 on sale. It runs on Windows, macOS and Linux, and on Steam Deck it's rated Playable, meaning it works, though not necessarily perfectly. There's full controller support, including DualShock and DualSense, 26 achievements, cloud saves and Family Sharing.

There's no Hebrew. The languages are English, Spanish, Portuguese, French, German, Japanese, Korean and Simplified Chinese, but it's an action game, and even players who don't read English will quickly figure out what to do. In the US it's rated E10+, suitable for ages 10 and up, for cartoon fantasy violence. There's also a free demo on Steam, which is the best way to check whether the difficulty suits whoever's sitting next to you on the couch.

## The bottom line

7/10

Castlebound is a lovely idea with an execution that's almost there. Defending a walking castle feels genuinely fresh, the papercraft art is among the prettiest I've seen in an indie game this year, and Arcade mode is pure fun. On the other hand, the start is slow and grindy, the wizard is overpowered, and co-op bosses are long and draining, in exactly the mode where the game should shine. For under ₪36, if you have a couch, a few controllers and friends who like to shout, it's an easy buy. For families with young kids, start with the demo.
:::

**What I liked**

- A walking castle: an original, charming idea
- Gorgeous papercraft art and catchy music
- Local co-op for up to four, with characters you can always spot
- An addictive, rewarding Arcade mode
- Low price and a free demo

**What I didn't**

- A slow start that feels like a grind
- Bosses with too much health in co-op
- The wizard is far stronger than the other heroes
- The knight unlocks late
- No Hebrew
